# History and Fun Facts of Paraguay: From the Toro Candil to Utopian Colonies

Paraguay is a country with a history so intense, tragic and singular that it often seems taken from a magical realism novel. Surrounded by continental giants and isolated for decades, this people has forged an iron identity capable of withstanding demographic catastrophes and rewriting its own social rules.

In this guide we gather the most surprising and little-known fun facts and historical milestones of deep Paraguay.

---

## 1. The Experiment of "El Supremo": The World's First Socialist State?

Long before Karl Marx wrote *The Communist Manifesto*, Paraguay tried out one of the most radical and controlled state economies in history. 

Under the dictatorship of **José Gaspar Rodríguez de Francia (1814-1840)**, known as "El Supremo" (The Supreme One):
* The country sealed itself off from trade and foreign influences.
* Most of the land and industry was nationalized.
* Higher education was banned, as were marriages within the same upper social class, in order to force intermixing between the Spanish elite and the indigenous population, seeking to eliminate hierarchies of race and class.
* Although the economy managed to become self-sufficient and stable, the regime of absolute isolation lasted almost three decades, until the dictator's death.

## 2. The Toro Candil: Bullfighting with Cows and Fire

Paraguay has its own version of Spanish bullfighting, with a very particular, animal-friendly twist:

* **Toro Candil:** It is practiced at traditional festivals in the rural interior (especially on the night of San Juan). 
* **The game:** Instead of aggressive bulls, cows are used and the game is played in total darkness, lighting the field with only the light of candles placed on the animals' horns (or using a wood-and-leather frame imitating a bull with lit horns, called a *toro candil*).
* **No blood:** Unlike traditional bullfighting, in the Toro Candil the animal is not harmed, wounded or killed; it is a game of agility and rural bravery.

## 3. The Demographic Catastrophe and the Polygamous Repopulation

The **War of the Triple Alliance (1864-1870)** plunged the country into the worst demographic catastrophe of the modern era in the Western Hemisphere. Facing the combined forces of Brazil, Argentina and Uruguay alone, Paraguay lost **60% - 70% of its total population**.

* **The shortage of men:** Historical sources indicate that up to **90% of the adult male population** died in the conflict or from the associated epidemics. In rural areas, the ratio reached 20 women for every surviving man.
* **Unofficial polygamy:** Faced with the imminent risk of the nation's extinction, the government and the Catholic Church turned a blind eye and encouraged informal polygamy and common-law unions. Priests were even de facto allowed to break celibacy to help repopulate the country. This historical milestone shaped Paraguayan family structures, explaining the high rate of de facto and informal unions in society today.

## 4. A Navy Without a Sea

Paraguay and Bolivia are the only countries in South America without direct access to the sea. Even so, Paraguay maintains one of the **largest river naval forces on the planet**, with more than 3,000 active personnel. 

This is because the river arteries of the Paraguay and Paraná rivers are the vital channels for export and import trade, connecting the country to the Atlantic Ocean through Argentine territory. The Paraguayan Navy carries out security patrols, fights smuggling and holds annual naval combat exercises.

## 5. The Paraguayan Chaco and the Mennonites

The **Gran Chaco** covers 60% of the country's territory but is home to less than 5% of its inhabitants. Historically known as the "Green Hell" for its extreme temperatures of over 45°C and long droughts, it was the setting of the **Chaco War (1932-1935)** against Bolivia, in a futile search for oil deposits that turned out not to exist.

* **The Mennonites:** In 1927, pacifist communities of German and Russian origin (Mennonites) settled in the inhospitable Chaco, receiving self-government concessions from the state.
* **The agricultural miracle:** Through cooperative work and adapted techniques, they tamed the Chaco soil and today their colonies (such as Fernheim, Neuland and Loma Plata) produce **almost half of the dairy products** in Paraguay and a large share of beef exports. They operate practically as autonomous mini-nations within the country, with their own schools, banks and internal social laws.

## 6. Failed Utopias of Australians and Aryans in the Jungle

At the end of the 19th century, Paraguay served as a blank canvas for strange sociological experiments by foreign settlers:

* **New Australia (1893):** Founded by 238 Australian socialists led by the journalist William Lane, who sought to create a utopian commune based on abstinence from alcohol, cooperative work and Anglo-Saxon purity. It failed quickly due to disputes over Lane's dictatorial leadership and the rigid prohibitions.
* **Nueva Germania (1887):** Founded by Elisabeth Förster-Nietzsche (sister of the famous German philosopher Friedrich Nietzsche) and her husband Bernhard Förster. They tried to create a vegetarian colony of supposed Aryan supremacy in the jungle of the San Pedro department. The tropical climate, pests and debts brought the colony to ruin, and Förster ended up taking his own life in a hotel in San Bernardino. German-speaking descendants still remain in the area today.
