# The Reality of Paraguay: Safety, Infrastructure and the Pros and Cons of Emigrating

In recent years Paraguay has positioned itself as one of South America's best-kept secrets for investors and expatriates. However, emigrating to any country in the world requires an honest analysis, with its lights and its shadows. Idealizing a destination or letting yourself be guided by extremely optimistic narratives is the perfect recipe for frustration.

In this report we break down Paraguay's most notable strengths in safety, but also the harsh challenges of its urban infrastructure, and how to assess whether this country truly fits your life plan.

---

## 1. Safety: The Region's Best-Kept Secret

When safety in Latin America is discussed, people usually talk about the improvements in El Salvador, the stability of Uruguay or the modernity of Chile. Paraguay is rarely mentioned, even though it **shows levels of tranquility and personal safety that far surpass the vast majority of its neighbors**:

* **Everyday peace:** Except in specific red zones of high precariousness (such as the emblematic **La Chacarita** neighborhood near downtown Asunción), violent crime or armed robbery is extremely unusual in the country's commercial and residential areas.
* **Police presence:** A high police visibility on the streets and in the financial districts of the main cities is common.
* **Comparative safety:** Expatriates from countries in the region (such as Argentina, Chile, Colombia or Mexico) point out that the peace of mind with which you can walk at night or leave your car parked in Paraguay is markedly superior and cannot be compared with the current context of widespread insecurity on the continent.

## 2. The Downsides: The Harsh Reality of Urban Infrastructure

While safety and taxation weigh strongly in Paraguay's favor, public infrastructure and urban planning are undoubtedly its greatest weakness. Those who travel expecting "Swiss clockwork" efficiency or the order of a European city will face a reality check:

* **Impassable sidewalks:** In Paraguay, **the municipality neither standardizes nor maintains sidewalks**; the neighbors themselves must build and repair them in front of their properties. The result is an uneven patchwork, full of cracks, bizarre steps and potholes that make walking comfortably difficult and are an insurmountable barrier for people with reduced mobility.
* **Cobblestones and lack of paving:** A great many side streets, and even avenues in cities of the interior, are made of ***empedrado*** (cobblestones or irregular stones laid by hand) or dirt roads. This causes constant vibration when driving and accelerates vehicle wear.
* **Potholes and flooding:** It is common to find deep holes or potholes in the asphalt that take months to be repaired. In addition, during heavy tropical rains, the lack of efficient storm drainage systems produces dangerous torrents of water (*raudales*) on streets and avenues.
* **Obsolete public transport:** The *colectivos* (buses) are usually in terrible condition, very worn out, and the conventional version runs without air conditioning. Cycling is dangerous because of the chaotic traffic and the absence of protected bike lanes.

## 3. Who Is Paraguay Right For, and Who Is It Not?

The key to success when migrating to Paraguay does not lie in whether the country is good or bad in itself, but in **how it fits your current life plan**:

### Who it DOES suit:
* **Entrepreneurs and freelancers:** Those looking for fiscal and economic **breathing room** to create and launch businesses without tax suffocation or excessive bureaucratic regulation.
* **Seekers of calm and family life:** Those who value close, warm human interaction, traditional values, a slow pace and a natural environment.
* **Retirees or people living on investment income:** People with income in foreign currency (euros or dollars) who want to multiply their purchasing power and live with high standards of comfort at low cost.

### Who it does NOT suit:
* **Rigid, highly structured profiles:** Those who need absolute predictability, uniformity in public order, perfect sidewalks to walk on and impeccable public transport.
* **First-world expectations:** Those who are not willing to accept that Paraguay is a developing country that lives with visible socioeconomic inequalities and administrative processes that sometimes depend more on contacts and human flexibility than on written procedure.
